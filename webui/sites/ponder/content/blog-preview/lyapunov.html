§ I. Mario Markus's λ-Diagrams

↩ contents

The Lyapunov fractal was introduced by Mario Markus under the supervision of Benno Hess in the 1989 paper Lyapunov exponents of the logistic map with periodic forcing. It is a visualization of the iterated logistic map fn(x)=rn·x·(1−x) with a periodically varying parameter rn taking on two values a,b according to a rule specified as a string of As and Bs. For instance the string ABA corresponds to the sequence r0=a,r1=b,r2=a,r3=a,r4=b,r5=a,… which repeats ad infinitum. The values of a and b vary with the pixel coordinates within the diagrams. Starting typically from x0=0.5, the Lyapunov exponent λ is computed for each such parametrization:

λ=limn→∞1n∑i=1nlog|f′i(xi)|

These exponents are then mapped to a color gradient. A negative exponent to indicates convergence of perturbed trajectories, while a positive exponent indicates divergence of perturbed trajectories. Speaking less preciselyi, this can be interpreted as stability vs. chaos. Often, these cases are colorized according to distinct schemes. The values are unboundedii, so some normalization is required.

The Lyapunov exponent is where the Lyapunov fractal gets its traditional name. Since Aleksandr Lyapunov never lived to see these fractals, they are sometimes called “Markus-Lyapunov fractals” instead. Markus himself, however, in his 2007 book Charts for Prediction and Chance,iii uses the term “λ-diagrams”, as λ is the traditional symbol used for Lyapunov exponents and strictly speaking not every such diagram is a fractal in a rigorous sense.iv Ultimately it's a subjective matter, but I regard Charts as the most authoritative source on the nomenclature.

At time of writing, most of the information one can find on the internet about the “Lyapunov fractal” focuses exclusively on the variants based on the logistic map. This is a tragedy, as the concept of Lyapunov exponent is much more general, and so the full aesthetic range of these diagrams often goes overlooked. As early as his 1990 publication Chaos in Maps with Continuous and Discontinuous Maxima, Markus explores a different, discontinuous modificationv of the logistic map, but it's in Charts that he really takes the concept and runs with it, applying the visualization technique across more than 39 different dynamical systems.

The λ-diagrams sit in the overlap between art and science. While they can be appreciated with or without care for their “meaning”, to engage with the underlying math is to experience their beauty more fully; and while these and related techniques can help us understand the dynamics of real physical phenomena, it would be sad to appreciate them solely for their utility.

def get_r(iteration):
    index = iteration % length(sequence)

def rolling_average(current_average, next_value, iteration):
    if is_nan_or_inf(current_average):

            log_derivative = log(abs(derivative))
            average_log_derivative = rolling_average(
                average_log_derivative,
                log_derivative,
                iteration - skip_iterations + 1

        x = x_next

    return colorize(average_log_derivative)

§ II. Understanding the Lyapunov Exponent

↩ contents

Behold the Lyapunov exponent:

λ=limn→∞1n∑i=1nlog|ddxfi(xi)|

It's my intent that by the end of this section most readers will find this formula to be a perfectly straightforward answer to a perfectly natural question; but to start our journey it's helpful to take a look at the kind of system the Lyapunov exponent is meant to help us understand. One such example, the function f(x)=r·x·(1−x), is known as the logistic map. Let's take a look at how this function behaves across the unit interval [0,1]:

In interactive plots, the bounds of the sliders have been set somewhat conservatively, for the sake of readers who may be using low-end devices. However, most of these limits are not enforced for the textual input elements. You may push your device to its limits, at your own peril.

Playing around with the parameters a bit, one can observe a number of basic facts:

  • For r from 0 to 4, the unit interval is mapped back into itself, so no matter how many times we apply the map, we don't have to worry about anything outside that range.
  • Since f(0)=0 and f(1)=0, those make for uninteresting starting values.
  • For small values of r, the unit interval (excluding the endpoints) converges on a single value.
  • As r increases, at some point there's a change, and each point on the interval eventually finds itself bouncing between two values, but when that happens those are the same two values, across the whole interval.
  • As r increases further, there are more values to bounce between, and by the time r is quite close to 4, the plot gets very messy very quickly and this ceases to be a useful visualization.

The most important takeaway from that visualization is that, as long as it's not 0 or 1, we could start from almost anyi x-value and the system's long-term dynamics would be qualitatively similar. So, let's not keep wasting a dimension of our visualization on that. Instead, let's take a single x0 and plot its behavior for many timesteps:

We can see here that indeed the long-term qualitative behavior of the system is not greatly impacted by our choice of x0. We can see more clearly now the periodic and aperiodic. If you turn up the iteration count, you'll see that plot becomes qualitatively monotonous in the long run in either case. So, plotting the entire timeseries is still a bit of a waste of a dimension in our visualization of this system. Perhaps we should sweep across values of r and view some kind of summary of the long-term behavior of the system under that parametrization.

Now we're getting somewhere. There is a lot going on here. This kind of figure is known as a bifurcation diagram, and the bifurcation diagram of the logistic map has been studied extensively. There's a good chance you've seen it before; maybe you've even seen a demonstration of how it relates to the Mandelbrot set. It is tempting to attempt to catalogue every interesting fact and feature of the bifurcation diagram, but this is an article about Lyapunov fractals, so let's stay on task.

In the context of our journey toward understanding the Lyapunov fractal, we are interested in a single simple question about each parametrization: is the trajectory stable, or chaotic? In a sense, the bifurcation diagram is telling us too much. We'd like to put a single number to each parametrization, characterizing just how tidy or untidy a system it defined. We can look at any slice of the bifurcation diagram and eyeball it, but how can we compute it?

Well, one idea would be to simply take the limit of the average of the logarithm of the magnitude of the derivative:

:)

Many of the explanations you can find online of the Lyapunov fractal would be happy to just drop this formula on you from on high and move on, but personally I think you'd be justified in demanding a little more explanation than that. The idea from which this measure (the maximal Lyapunov exponent, or MLE) arises is that of defining the quantity we're after as sensitivity to initial conditions. If we give the system a tiny nudge at the start, how much do we expect that to change the result, hundreds of iterations later? If you return to the previous visualizations and toy with them for a bit, you can see that while the long term behavior of the system is qualitatively largely independent of x0, this idea of how sensitive the specific values at any point late in the sequence are to slight changes in x0 lines up well with our looser intuitive notions of apparent-order-versus-apparent-chaos.

λ=limn→∞1n∑i=1nlog|ddxfi(xi)|

So then we just need to quantify this sensitivity. Let's consider just one iteration. How sensitive is x1=f(x0) to a change in x0? Well, this is exactly what the differentiation operator ddx is for. Speaking a bit loosely, we denote "the rate at which b changes, proportionate to a tiny change in a" as ddab. Since the quantity we are after is the change in xn induced by a tiny change in x0, what we are looking for is ddx0xn.

Following a common convention, from here on we will denote the derivative of a function with a tick mark, like so: f′≔ddxf. The logistic map is a polynomial function, and there is a well known straightforward procedure for obtaining the derivatives of polynomials. As we are interested only in this one function, we won't be diving into the methodological details. For f(x)=r·x·(1−x), the derivative is f′(x)=r−2·r·x

Let's start with the case of x1. We have ddx0x1=f′(x0).

Now, how about x2? Well, we have x2=f(x1)=f(f(x0)) and thus ddx0x2=ddx0f(x1)=ddx0f(f(x0)). We must be more careful than simply replacing f with f′ in a nested case like this, and instead apply something called the chain rule:

ddxa(b(x))=a′(b(x))·b′(x)

In our case, this evaluates to:

ddx0f(f(x0))=f′(f(x0))·f′(x0)=f′(x1)·f′(x0)

It is left as an exercise for the reader to prove that the pattern holds:

ddx0xn=∏i=0n−1f′(xi)≔f′(xn−1)·f′(xn−2)·…·f′(x1)·f′(x0)

Now, we are interested in whether and to what degree this quantity grows or shrinks as the number of iterations increases. There happens to be an exceedingly useful mathematical tool for modeling growth and decay, known as the exponential function, denoted as eitherii exp(x) or ex. The exponential of a negative value tends toward zero, while the exponential of a positive value grows.

Since the exponential of a real value is always positive, we will have to take the absolute value of our derivative if we want to model it as an exponential. This is fine, as what we really care about is the magnitude. Calling the rate of exponential growth or decay λn at iteration n, we have:

en·λn=ddx0xn=∏i=0n−1|f′(xi)|

Now, since the (natural) logarithm is the inverse of the exponential function, we can take the log of both sides and obtain:

λn=1nlog∏i=0n−1|f′(xi)|

(It is worth noting that the original Markus paper actually uses a base-2 logarithm; this is equivalent to simply applying a constant scaling factor.) Now, this is already a nice formula for λn, but if we try to do actual computation with it, the product will be numerically unstable. However it is a well-known and very useful algebraic property of logarithms that the logarithm of a product is a sum of logarithms:

λn=1n∑i=0n−1log|f′(xi)|

Now if we just ask "what does this λn tend toward as n grows?", we have recovered our formula from earlier:

λ=limn→∞1n∑i=mn−1log|f′(xi)|

You may have noticed that I snuck a variable m in there in place of zero. In practice it is convenient to skip the first several iterations when computing this value, as the system can take a little while to settle into its limiting behavior, and there are some pesky issues with the very first iteration in particular (more on this in the subsection on "Algebraic Curves" in section IV).

§ IV. Observations of the Logistic λ-Diagram

↩ contents

The original paper concerns itself only with A and B values in the range [0,4]. However, there is no technical constraint that prevents us from peeking outside that box. When we do, it becomes visually apparent that this range is not entirely arbitrary (the logistic map remains finite there). The crisp edge of this "blessed" square stands out prominently. Nevertheless, there is some interesting structure outside of it. Some of the superstable curves poke out into the void of divergence. A smaller, distorted square exists where A and B are both negative.

Algebraic Curves

Qualitatively, the fractal appears to be structured along a "skeleton" of precise curves, the so-called "superstable" curves, where the Lyapunov exponent diverges to −∞. We can actually produce exact algebraic descriptions of these curves!

Consider the formula for the Lyapunov exponent: limn→∞1n∑i=1nlog|ddxfi(xi)|

Since log|x| diverges to −∞ as x approaches 0, we have that the entire sum must diverge when ddxfn(xn)=0. Given x0=12, we can apply the logistic formula with alternating parameters a and b repeatedly and obtain ddxfn(xn) as a multivariate polynomial in a and b.

Because x0=12, the first iteration of our iterative process actually has derivative 0 uniformly across all r-values, which is why the sum in our Lyapunov exponent calculation begins at index 1 instead of 0. Anyway, assuming the basic "AB" sequence, and choosing scaling factors cn so as to produce polynomials with the same zeros but with integer coefficients, the first few curves Pn=cn·ddxfn(xn)=0 are as follows:

P1=−a+2=0P2=a2b−4ab+8=0P3=a5b2−8a4b2+16a3b2+16a3b−64a2b+128=0P4=a10b5−16a9b5+96a8b5+32a8b4−256a7b5−384a7b4+256a6b5+1536a6b4+256a6b3−2048a5b4−1792a5b3+2048a4b3+4096a3b3+4096a3b2−16384a2b2+32768=0

While the first few of these can be worked out by hand (an exercise for the reader, of course), they grow in complexity very quickly. By the sixth iteration, there are already hundreds of terms, and by the tenth, a plaintext file containing the formula takes up several megabytes. I had an LLM agent use SymPy to produce the first ten iterations of polynomials, and while I didn't look at the code at all, the task was straightforward and the plots look correct:

You can see how these curves accumulate and form the "backbone" of the fractal in the interactive renderer by setting "skip" to 1 and "iterations" to 2, and then gradually incrementing the iteration count. The negative-infinite exponents pleasingly trace out exactly these algebraic curves. It's also interesting to note the alternation between two different qualitative types of curve on even and odd iterations. I have not put much thought into that, but I'm sure there's an interesting reason for it.

Of course, in the original papers, the first 600 iterations are skipped. Some of the algebraic curves from early on are lost due to this, but others reassert themselves no matter how late the accumulation begins. Indeed even with "skip" set to 1 in the renderer you can see that many of the lines appear to fade away quite quickly as the iteration count grows. In reality the limit does still diverge at every point on any of these curves, it's just that the ones that fade have vanishing influence on nearby points. The curves that very visibly remain are those that are included in infinitely many later curves. If it is only these "limiting superstable curves" one cares about, however, there is no finite number of iterations we can skip to exclude all the rest. But I don't feel like pursuing that line of thought any further here and now.

Anyway, I think this is a pretty neat decomposition of much of the structure of the fractal.

Presets

Presets in the interactive renderer have been set up to approximately reproduce the figures from Markus's 1989 and 1990 papers. The renders are not 100% faithful, but they are best-effort. The papers specify the r-value sequences and the coordinate bounds of each render. They do not specify the starting offset in the sequence, which, as I discovered in the course of implementing the presets, is not always 0. This page's interactive renderer has a variable width and height, so I calculated a center point and zoom value that would approximately match the original bounds. It may be necessary to resize one's window, zoom, and pan around, in order to get a more exact match to what is present in the original papers. Some of the original renders also exhibit anisotropic stretching. My renderer does not at present have the capacity to replicate this effect. The original papers specify the x0 values for many of the figures, but I found that some visual features (for example, in 1989 Fig. 1(a)) could not be reproduced without modifying x0 slightly. I opted to make this modification, prioritizing visual similarity with the original visualization over precise copying of parameters.
