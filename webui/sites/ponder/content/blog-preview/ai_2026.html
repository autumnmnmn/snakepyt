AI 2026

Where we are, and where we aren't

In October 2026, when I began writing this, OpenAI had just released a massive GitHub repo of .pdf and .lean files.

There were more than 700 PDFs, and a substantial subset of them were even associated with Lean certificates.

This led to quite a bit of discussion. Is it the end of human mathematics? Is it time to move to a “math 2.0”? Is AGI here? Or ASI? The eschaton?

With all the commentary flying around, much of it quite bombastic, I figured I should go ahead and make a record of where my head was at at the time. In the process of writing, the scope of the post expanded quite a bit beyond just mathematics. It's become I guess an attempt at making legible a considerable portion of my thinking on AI more broadly. Those who follow me on BlueSky and are pious enough to read all my posts every day will probably already have seen many of these takes, albeit with a little less polish and context. In keeping with the aphoristic spirit of a poster, I don't intend this as a persuasive piece, more of a snapshot of my thoughts. I have structured it as a dialogue with a fictional interrogator, whose primary purpose is to bring up various points I've seen on social media that I want to address here, and whose secondary purpose is to say whatever is most convenient for me so I can take the “conversation” wherever I want it to go.

While I was assembling these thoughts I ended up reading William Thurston's famous essay On Proof and Progress in Mathematics, and as it turns out some parts of what I wanted to say about mathematics were quite similar to what Thurston had already capably put to words before I was even born. While I hadn't read it myself before I wrote up the bulk of this, my thinking has doubtlessly been greatly influenced by it, albeit indirectly. Anyway, I think it's quite good, and its author has significantly more “math cred” than I probably ever will. If you only have time to read one essay today, I encourage you to go read that one.

Finally, I must note that when I was nearing the end of the editing of this piece, Levent Alpöge announced a claim of a Claude-assisted and Lean-formalized proof of the notorious Riemann Hypothesis. Regardless of how reviews of it shake out, I can already say I appreciate Alpöge's general approach to communicating the result much more than I appreciate OpenAI's, for reasons I think will be made clear enough by what I've already written.

§ I. AI Mathematics

↩ contents

What do you think of all these results?

There is a large amount of content in OpenAI's repo. Many of the “results” are at this point in time only PDFs. Some have Lean certificates, many do not. The results without Lean certificates are lengthy, and in many cases appear inscrutable. The few expert assessments that I've seen so far give mostly negative impressions of the quality of the writing, though I have also seen some say that the core ideas are legible enough. As far as the timeline of the production of the papers is concerned, however, it is basically not possible that OpenAI performed any meaningful review of the non-Lean-certified results prior to their publication: there is simply too much content there, created in a short window of time before its release, all of a sort that would take a long time to comb over even if it were written clearly, which it wasn't. So, for the non-certified results, I have wondered since the day they dropped what it is that makes OpenAI confident they're worth sharing. I should note that at time of writing, OpenAI has already retracted three papers due to a sign error in one of them. Perhaps OpenAI has released the unconfirmed stuff because they just don't have the ability to evaluate their model's results internally any more and would like some free training signal from the mathematical community at large.

So, a verdict on the non-certified results: basically nobody knows, at time of writing, how many of them are slop, including OpenAI, and it may take a lot of time and effort to answer that. They are worth digging into only if it is to sate one's own genuine curiosity; certainly they do not rise to the typical standards of expository quality that obligate mathematicians working in the relevant areas to take a long thoughtful look.

Well some of them are formalized in Lean! What about those ones?

Those results are more interesting, especially if you're the kind of person who's very interested in autoformalization. Unfortunately that's not me. I think autoformalization is a neat area of math and I'm glad people are working on it, but I'm also skeptical of the ambitions of foundationalist projects in general. More on that later.

But isn't a Lean certificate the Gold Standard in math?

I have seen people declaring that to be the case. I just don't think it is or ever has been true, at least not outside of a relatively narrow subset of mathematicians. Perhaps some of those working on Lean or similar tools would like it to be the case going forward. Funny enough, math has for some time already been plagued with a pop-cultural misunderstanding that centers ZFC set theory in that Gold-Standard role that Lean with its type-theoretic foundations is now supposed to fill, as though it had already caught on as the foundation of all of mathematics. Now, I am not an academic, so take this with a grain of salt, but my impression is that most mathematicians don't proceed from dependent type theoretic nor ZFC set-theoretic axioms in their work, and don't feel that formalizing their work in Lean nor in ZFC would be a particularly important thing for them to do, and that very few mathematicians judge results primarily or even at all on the basis of the presence or absence of a Lean certificate.

Well maybe they should! Wouldn't it be good if every result in math came with a machine verification?

I don't think so. I find that quite dystopian. Making sure a formalization actually means anything is hard, for one thing. It is not enough to just have Claude Code tell you that your result is certified by its vibecode. At the very least you need to learn enough Lean to confirm that the theorem statement is what you intend. And at that point, every mathematician, even those working well outside of dependent type theory, will have been forced to define all their work at all times on its terms, rather than the terms native to their own area. And furthermore even if we posit that LLMs handle all the hard work, then the imposition of a standard of machine verification for all mathematical results would amount to little more than a tithe for mathematicians or their employers to pay a handful of LLM providers in order to be able to satisfy bureaucratic standards.

But what if LLMs get so smart that mathematicians don't need to think about the Lean stuff at all and can just trust that the LLM is always absolutely right, and the inference for models of that caliber gets literally too cheap to meter?

You can dream your dream, I guess. I think most of my points hold even in such a hypothetical, but I don't find it to be connected enough to reality to be worth splitting hairs about.

Ok, let's take a step back. As you said it's important to verify the theorem statement is right. But once you've done that, you can be sure your proof is right, right?

Wrong. Even assuming that the theorem as stated in Lean is the right theorem, and the Lean formalization of the proof checks out, the correspondence between the argument you intend to formalize and the Lean formalization of that argument must be established, and Lean cannot help you with that. It's a difficult task, especially for advanced results. A preprint on arXiv already purports to have identified mismatches between the argument laid out in OpenAI's Finite Time Blowup for Navier-Stokes paper and its formalization; the same preprint also discusses the formal complexity of the general problem of establishing such a correspondence, arguing that it is, in a precise technical sense, pretty dang hard.

Ok so maybe we can't be sure that the PDFs explain the proof right. But we at least know that the theorem is true, right?

(bong rip) Depends what you mean by “know” and “true”.

...elaborate?

One thing we must consider is the trustworthiness of Lean. For most intents and purposes you're probably going to be fine trusting Lean. But at time of writing, the most recent discovery of soundness bugs in Lean was in August 2026. I have been told by people with relevant expertise that the bugs involved were not of the sort that could easily be squirreled away in an innocent-looking proof. There are also multiple kernels; using these provides a degreei of defense-in-depth against kernel soundness issues. I still find the very existence of this kind of issue to be cause for caution. The most recent bugs were found by an audit by an internal OpenAI agent tasked with probing for vulnerabilities. I think frontier-scale RL training in which the model's reward signal may be tied to the verifier output is another tier of threat entirely beyond that when it comes to sniffing out vulnerabilities in the tool; I think that is in fact the biggest test to which Lean has ever been put. And while it may well be that Lean holds up to this test and all the certified proofs stay standing indefinitely, I think it's reasonable at this time to treat it as at least within the realm of possibility that some results might not hold up. On the other hand I have also seen people lodge predictions that the results are definitely bullshit and I take that too to be wildly overconfident.

Now, we can enumerate all the assumptions one must make in order for a Lean-certified result to hold, and attempt to quantify their probabilities, and so on. And the resultant probability when we multiply it all out might well be quite high, but it is crucially not identically 1.

But then, the same can be said for many ordinary mathematical proofs, no? The numbers may even turn out to be less favorable for human mathematics than for Lean!

Indeed! In ordinary mathematics we don't escape the abject epistemic situation of subjective existence; our proofs are contingent on the sound proof-checking performed by our own minds, or at least those of mathematicians and their reviewers. Consider Alpöge's claimed RH proof. Before we had that, we already had numerical results demonstrating 1013 nontrivial zeros all on the critical line. Insofar as we are thinking about probability and epistemology, it may be that “rationally” we already should have believed RH was true on that basis, even without it constituting a rigorous formal proof. So what does Alpöge's gigabyte of Lean do for us? That's not a rhetorical question: I don't mean to deny that it does something for us, but what that is is more subtle than just giving us credence that the hypothesis holds. We already had credence. What we want is not merely to know that it is true but to really experience that truth in some way. The differences between proofs, broadly construed, are not entirely captured by multiplying out probabilities and quantifying uncertainty. There are more dimensions at play, thus far we have only moved from the black and white of truth and falsehood to the grayscale of uncertainty. But we are still missing color. There is a rich world of epistemic and phenomenological diversity among the things we call “proofs”, and I think the attitude that “machine verification is all you need” is indicative of a totally different philosophy of proof than I subscribe to.

So what is proof about, to you?

Well I think to try and answer that in one tidy definition is folly, but let me try to do so anyway. Proof is about what is sufficient to produce shared conviction among skeptical minds; or, the point of proving things is to grant our fellow investigators access to our understanding not just as some finished product, but as a process of systematic investigation of some matter. A number of different methods of proof are valuable in a number of different situations. Many formal and informal systems of proof can coexist, and we don't particularly need to pick out any formalism and treat it as “the foundation of all of math”. Formalization and rigor aren't the same thing, and rigor isn't everything anyway. To encode some particular symbolic system of inference and have a computer check proofs in it is an interesting project, one I'm glad there are people working on, but insofar as its aim is “foundational” I take it to be misguided.

That's all pretty abstract.

Let's get more concrete, then. We already talked about some new proofs of major results, but for contrast let's look at something much simpler: a proof that a rational number's product with itself cannot be 2. One way to prove this is to show that the presumption that if we have integers p and q in reduced form (that is, sharing no factors) such that (pq)2=2 entails a contradiction. To see this, we can rearrange to p2=2q2, note that this makes p2 even, and that for p2 to be even means p must be even as well. From p having a factor of 2, we see that p2 must actually have a factor of 4, that is to say there is some k such that p2=4k and substituting this into our earlier rearrangement we have 4k=2q2 and thus 2k=q2. But now we see q2 is even, and so q must be even as well. This is the point at which things fall apart: we have that p and q share a factor (namely, 2): they are not in reduced form after all. This contradicts our starting assumption, that there exists a rational number in reduced form that squares to 2. If such a number existed, then we would have a contradiction.

Now, there are things I've left you to take on faith in that proof, but they are few, and easily identified and investigated even by one without a particularly deep background in mathematics. You might wonder “How do you know that a number that squares to an even number must itself be even?” or “Why can't there be a rational that doesn't have a reduced form?” or (if you're particularly astute)“What's wrong with having a contradiction?”

As far as the first two questions go, if you're genuinely curious about them, I encourage you to ask an LLM. Even a free tier model can almost certainly reproduce the rigorous form of this proof that one would encounter in a typical course on mathematical logic. For the third case, I encourage you to read up on dialetheism, paraconsistency, and inconsistent mathematics and become the bane of classical logic bros.

But anyway, when we compare this proof to OpenAI's Navier-Stokes blow-up result or Alpöge's RH, we find a world of difference. The prerequisites to fully understanding the claims being made are significant, before we even get into how it is to be demonstrated. Being able to verify that the formulation of the theorem statement in Lean is correct is another challenge entirely. One must have faith in the experts who attest to that, or perhaps divert oneself to years of study to become one. And then there is the proof itself, a monumental amount of code to examine, more or less inscrutable depending on one's prior familiarity with Lean and with the mathematical concepts at play in the proof. But one is not expected to care about the content of the proof, only that the Lean kernels all agree that it establishes the theorem. So, one must have faith in this software engineering project, Lean 4. Now, this is a project that very thoughtful people have put a lot of work into, and the use of multiple different kernels to verify the proof does provide a degree of reassurance; rather than having faith in the correctness of one dependent type theoretic proof checker, one needs only have faith that all these kernels don't happen to be wrong in the very same way, with this proof happening to exploit that wrongness. And to be clear, I do not intend to insinuate that that faith is misplaced.

We put faith in engineering projects all the time, including software engineering projects. Sometimes this comes back to bite us, but most of us go our entire lives without anyone we know being killed by the collapse of a dam, bridge, or building. The only engineers who fail us routinely enough to earn a significant rank on a chart of typical causes of death are traffic engineers.

Anyway, when it comes to advanced results, for instance a Wiles' proof of Fermat's Last Theorem, the character of the proof may appear to a typical layperson (or even a mathematician not working in the same area) more akin to the daunting Lean artifact than to the elementary proof of the irrationality of root 2. There is a lot of trust involved, for most of us, in any of these results. We trust that the mathematicians are scrupulous in their investigations and that their reviewers are appropriately diligent. Some want to portray autoformalization as removing totally the human element, the element of having to trust other people in order to know something is true. Software engineering culture is presently obsessed with tools that “eliminate entire classes of errors”, with a stubborn reluctance to talk about the tradeoffs involved in doing so. The imperfection of human reasoning is blown up into a thoroughgoing contempt for human reason, a sort of misanthropic denialism of the capabilities of the human mind. But the trust is still there, unavoidably; it is only placed differently. The difference has qualitative and quantitative aspects that both matter, and how much trust one must extend varies from person to person, depending on their own background and on what kind of certitude or understanding they want or need.

As I mentioned before, we do not simply want to know that a result holds, we want to experience that truth. I want to make that concrete as well. Consider the Navier-Stokes blowup. Why do we care about this? The Clay Mathematics Institute's statement of the problem only seeks a knowing-that, it stipulates nothing about any insight or experience that might come from a proof which satisfies this. But one would be engaging in a sort of malicious compliance if one does not consider at all the question of why this is a Millennium Prize problem, why anyone cares to seek such a proof. The offering of the prize, the formalization of the task at hand, is an attempt at tracking what we care about, it is not itself the thing we care about. Simply knowing that a blowup can be induced, because the Lean kernel says so, does not give us very much of what we want, because what we want is to understand the character of the inherent pathology of the equations which enables the blowup. But then, as anyone that has followed the priority kerfuffle surrounding the release of OpenAI's blowup knows, mathematicians already had an inkling of this understanding, and within the community it was generally expected that there was a path to the answer, and indeed there were rumors that someone had set out already on that final stretch. So what exactly did OpenAI accomplish for mathematics by, upon hearing that the way to the summit might not be so far off, spending far more than the prize was worth in order to be the ones to get there first and plant their little flag? It seems to me a rather excessive exercise in missing the point.

Earlier you said “formalization isn't rigor”. You don't get to just declare that and move on to the next thing. Explain yourself!

Rigor is a quality, or vague cluster of properties, that proofs can have. To give a quick provisional definition, it's about being thorough, accounting for every special case, tracking every assumption one makes, spelling out every step of one's reasoning. Formalization is a class of ways of expressing proofs. Formalization isn't what the process of proving things “really is,” it's an attempt at precisely specifying sufficient criteria to ensure that rigor is being upheld. Some formal systems have properties that make them extremely useful in mathematics, especially when it comes to communicating rigorous proofs; formal systems are also themselves a subject of mathematical inquiry, and learning about them is vital to a mathematical education. However they are not the foundation of all mathematical practice. They are especially unsuited to form an epistemological foundation, for reasons both philosophical and technical.

There is also a glut of formal foundations to choose from. Supposing you want to be very serious about which foundations you proceed from, you could end up spending your entire lifetime just cataloguing their properties to inform your decision. And that doesn't just affect which statements you've proved, it affects what it even means for you to have proved them.

Wait, what do you mean by that?

Well, when you prove things in mathematics, there's a certain philosophical view which I think is outdated and mistaken but is still pretty common, which is that one is establishing eternal absolute truths in some very broad sense. But with formal mathematical results, “truth” is a formal term; it is not the same thing as truth as such, a topic which belongs not to math but dwells instead deep in the murky waters of philosophy; a logician's “truth” is like a graph theorist's “tree”ii. And what is actually shown when you prove some claim in some formal system is only that that claim is an entailment of the axioms and rules of inference of that system. Relatedly there's a prevalent notion that one might call something like “axiom-dogmatism”, the confusion of axioms and dogmas; that is to say, there are those who think of axioms as a set of fundamental beliefs one must hold in order to do math. Again that's not so common among mathematicians, it's a misunderstanding of mathematics, and one that makes less and less sense the more mathematics one engages with. Axioms are adopted situationally, provisionally; they're more a matter of definitions than facts. One axiom of groups is that they have identity elements. This isn't a true fact floating about in some realer-than-real Platonic realm, it's not a principle one must believe in in order to engage in reasoning, it's just part of what we mean when we say “group” in a mathematical context. In the context of group axioms it's pretty uncontroversial that axiom-dogmatism is a stifling confusion. When it comes to foundations and especially classical logic, however, there are some who find it hard to let go of -- though those are not typically the people actually working in those areas today. The main takeaway from all this is that if we are to be maximally pedantic, one's choice of what axiom system to prove things in is not simply a question of how to go about demonstrating the results: it changes what it even means to have demonstrated them.

Anyway, while I have more thoughts about formalism which I may write about one day, this blog post is supposed to be about AI, so let's get back to that.

If not proof, what is it that constitutes mathematical progress? What is the point of doing math?

Mathematics is a lot of things to a lot of people. There are a lot of ideas being thrown around right now about what math is “about” or “for”. But the very question of what constitutes progress smuggles in an assumption that mathematics as a practice is about“progress”, or that it needs to justify itself on extrinsic terms, and that is an assumption I don't agree with. To me, math is a friend; for me, the ideal engagement with it is dialectical. There is a give and take, it's a bit of an improv routine.

What do you think of Grant Sanderson's idea of centering “motivated explanation”?

Well, I think there's something a little funny in saying “maybe the thing we should value most in math is the thing I specialize in”, but at least he's charmingly self-aware about that, and ultimately I do think motivated explanation is something genuinely very valuable. In fact I think that forms a part of the gradual digestion of mathematical results which is perhaps one of the most important processes within mathematics but which occurs across generations. The development of mathematical pedagogy is severely discursively undervalued, even as in the wake of these AI results there are plenty of mathematicians who have been trying to articulate its value. What once was understood only barely and by a small handful of experts we now expect high schoolers to learn as a matter of course. That's a remarkable development, and it took a lot of work by a lot of people across a very long time to make that happen. But it's hard to track every participant in that work, and its breakthroughs are subtle and tend to go uncelebrated; and yet how much of what we have today depends on that work? It's hard to even imagine the counterfactual. The world would be too different in too many ways for us to say much of anything about it.

There's one quote from Sanderson in a guest post on Tao's blog that I want to critique just a little:

One clear shortcoming of a motivated explanation is that its validity is not binary the way a proof’s is. This is a big reason proof is so useful a way to measure progress: You can clearly define what does and does not have a proof yet. There will never be Lean for motivated explanations.

Sanderson cedes too much ground here: this is not a shortcoming of motivated explanation. This is a shortcoming in the ambient ideological expectation that everything should be evaluable by “objective” binary judgements. And furthermore there is a shortcoming in the way we individualize accreditation of progress, as our progress understood in fullness is not an individual achievement at all but a collective one.

Any thoughts on Terence Tao's “Math 2.0”?

I've been keeping up with much of what Tao has been saying in the wake of OpenAI's antics, and I find a lot of it very sensible and prudent. I also appreciate that he's been sharing his platform with a number of less famous mathematicians via guest posts on his blog. However I am of the opinion that the name“math 2.0” has unfortunate connotations; it's ceding too much ground! Mathematical practice will certainly be affected by AI, but we do not need to fundamentally change what math is for it to survive the incorporation of AI tools. Maybe the norms and practices of academic mathematicians need a reboot, but math itself does not need a “new major version”. Math 1.0 is vast, it contains multitudes.

Don't we lose something important, when we have answers handed to us without having to work for them?

Sure, I think there's an element of truth in some formulations of that idea, but I think that needs to be balanced against a frank acknowledgement that almost all the answers in math are handed to us already, and that that's actually great. I was born into a world where math is already a mature field of study. My entire formal education in math consisted of people more or less directly handing me answers to questions I might never have even thought to ask on my own. Truly independently doing all the work for myself, maybe I could have intuited some basic geometry.

So even though there is truth in this, it is not really my greatest concern. I love it when I find my questions already answered! But usually in such cases there is more than just an answer, and I think that's important. There are entire new ways of thinking about things, there are legible connections to other things I know which let me know them more deeply than before, and so on. I don't think I would find it very satisfying to just get “yes, it's proved, the proof is a massive library of Lean produced by an optimization routine, you'll never be able to make heads or tails of it”. As with the earlier talk of malicious compliance with the concept of proof, I feel there's significantly less value to me in a proof where all I can get out of it is “the formalization exists, and Lean says it checks out”.

So many of these results are proving nasty, inelegant, ugly things. Maybe math isn't beautiful, maybe math is ugly.

I've seen people say things along those lines. That's a bizarre reaction to the recent AI math stuff, in my opinion. I don't know how someone who previously genuinely thought math was beautiful could stop thinking so on the basis of those results; none of what I find beautiful in math has been lost or at all tarnished. Sure, I don't find those particular results beautiful. There are a lot of theorems I don't find particularly beautiful or interesting in math. I don't think very many people who have any sense for “mathematical beauty” were ever of the opinion that every theorem provable in Lean is beautiful.

Maybe aesthetic taste is a human weakness. Maybe it's what's kept us from learning all there is to learn. We miss out on important answers because we just can't stand how ugly the truth is.

I don't buy this, because these results are actually very much in line with the kind of stuff people have been churning out for a while. Galactic-runtime algorithms, bounds proofs with nasty arbitrary constants, &c; these are nothing new. And I also don't think the sheer quantity of them out there to be found is surprising; that kind of problem has basically been something you can go grind against and have a decent guarantee you'll come across some kind of result, inelegant as it might be. OpenAI's results I guess just shine a light on how much of contemporary academic math is just “go swing a pickaxe in the results mine”.

I guess being personally not part of academia, and viewing “math” as something broader than “churn out papers” makes it easier for me to view the ripping apart of those ore-rich hills by a bucket-wheel excavator as something less than apocalyptic.

And now the miners are all clamoring to explain how the real value of miners is telling people about mining. Ha.

Well the “ore” in this metaphor is something of questionable use-value anyway, and the “publish or perish” grind is just one case of the broader societal problem where perverse incentives lead every form of production to become slop production. The perverse incentives in question are largely induced by an ideology that demands constant legible productivity, even if that productivity isn't doing anyone much good, and backs this demand with the threat of being demoted to a position of dependency on the grace of another to survive, or if such grace cannot be secured, being left out on the street to die of exposure in the winter. The panicked responses of academic mathematicians are the appeals of people being asked “Ok, so if we can just roll up with a big machine and rip the hill apart for all the ore... why shouldn't society just discard you all, toss you all in a landfill?” But I think there's already much more to math than the “mining”. As a society we don't even “buy” the “ore”. Mathematical publications are not something purchased in bulk for industrial applications: the ore metaphor has been stretched too far. To strip away all metaphor: OpenAI has effectively decided to try and boost their own perceived value via a maneuver that diminishes the perceived value of the professional practice of mathematics. Much of the media environment took it at face value right off the bat. Mathematicians are correct to perceive this attack for what it is, and are prudent to scramble to produce a defense and present alternative narratives in the media. However, as this is a struggle concerned especially with public perception of their field, it is not so ideal for them to be seen to be panicking.

So you don't think OpenAI has made a model that's better at math than humans?

In the broadest sense, no. OpenAI has made a model that is better at a narrow slice of mathematical practice. This happens, from time to time. The number of mathematical tasks the computer can perform reliably, and faster than a human, was already quite large.

But isn't this all so shocking?

I don't know how ppl have it in them to get breathlessly hyped once per news cycle about AI for years on end as if every single development is a generation-defining breakthrough! Don't get me wrong, I can understand being impressed with advancements in AI, but the really big ones happened years ago! At a certain point, one should incorporate that fact into one's understanding of where we're at and stop being shocked and awed over and over again. I feel I already got the hype out of my system in the early days of LLMs (and diffusion/flow models, which, to be honest, I have always found more theoretically interesting, despite the industry's decision to optimize such models primarily toward the production of artless schlock).

Don't these results have such a mysterious, alien aura?

Sure, but that's nothing new. Much of the frontier of human mathematics already has an alien character. Just look at nlab. Or a quaternionic Julia set.

But you have to admit these models are impressive!

Yes. I do! I find them very impressive and useful. I think they're especially a boon to curious minds. Even with free-tier models, you can talk about some vague idea and the model can tell you there's a well known term for that, there's this or that relevant literature, &c. You can describe some strange notation or some difficulty that's been bothering you and even if you don't get a perfect answer it'll be a hell of a lot better than what google ever could have given you. And I find the models useful for doing quick experiments or visualizations that otherwise wouldn't have felt worth the effort; for those without programming skills who perceive the creation of visualizations as prohibitively difficult, they don't just reduce the effort, they make the formerly-impossible trivial. There's a lot to be excited about!

I genuinely think the way LLMs can already help people that want to learn the math that already exists is more revolutionary than the results they've recently proved in Lean. There's a quote in Thurston's Proof and Progress essay that stood out to me:

Basic concepts used every day within one subfield are often foreign to another subfield. Mathematicians give up on trying to understand the basic concepts even from neighboring subfields, unless they were clued in as graduate students.

This is something I think LLMs can help address. LLMs are an excellent resource for demystifying impenetrable jargon, and for pointing out to you when someone already has words for something you're trying to say.

And that's part of why I think if we had models being trained basically exactly the same way as OpenAI's but the opt-in “train on my shit” checkbox was off by default and users could have a greater regulatory assurance it was even being respected, and this training was being done by an institution controlled by the public, accessible as a public service, it'd be uncomplicatedly cool as hell. Everything everyone is working on instantly absorbed into a common pool of knowledge accessible to all, as fast as they can develop their ideas. The potential of the technology is, just as with many other technologies, utopian when it is detached from the socioeconomic context in which it actually exists. But what we actually have is a company controlled by Sam Altman and shareholders, it is an entity inclined to try to compete for research priority with its users, and to claim all the benefit for what is effectively a collective achievement, models built on collective human knowledge. It's dystopian that access to such a system is gated behind a subscription model or insanely-priced business-to-business bullshit. And then for it to be endlessly marketed as the soon-to-be replacement for all intellectual labor, or perhaps all labor, and as having transcended through scale alone its nature such that the latest LLM cannot simply be understood as yet another LLM, no, the latest and greatest model, we are always supposed to believe, has undergone the mysterious phenomenon of “emergence” and become something ineffably more (but no, we're not going to make that rigorous)-- is just tedious!

Ok fine. We're in section two now. So, you wouldn't call it AGI. What is it then, “just linear algebra”?

No. Linear algebra is important for understanding model architectures, but the nonlinearities are also quite important. But that's just a small nitpick: pointwise nonlinear functions are not prohibitively hard to understand, and the spirit of the dismissal “it's just linear algebra” is along the lines “all the math involved in machine learning is pretty trivial”. To say it's “just a bunch of matrix multiplication” is to conjure up a mental image of a huge array of numbers being multiplied and added according to some simple rules; the conceit of dismissing ML as an unsophisticated field on the basis of one's ability to picture that and to comprehend those simple rules is unfounded. For one thing, that's an “understanding” of ML that has very little explanatory power. There's nothing in the “it's just linear algebra” picture that makes it make sense that this process should be able to do the things models do. Why should it be possible to coax “a bunch of matmuls” into emitting plausible-to-native-speakers natural language replies to off-the-cuff queries, or emitting novel photorealistic images matching a user-specified description, or emitting a library of Lean code that proves theorems humans did not already have proofs of? Understanding matrix multiplication is about as helpful for answering these questions as understanding pointer arithmetic is for answering questions about the Linux kernel: it may have some relevance in some cases but it is not nearly enough on its own.

Setting aside the fact that it takes more than you'd learn in a typical linear algebra course to understand the implementations and, more importantly, the purposes of several important components of modern architectures, and setting aside the details of training dynamics and choice of optimizers, and the empirical study of how to get the most bang for your buck when deciding how big of a model to train and how long to train it and how much data you need, and setting aside the messy engineering of quantized computations and all the other optimizations involved in getting it all to work efficiently on real hardware, even when we have swept all that under the rug such that we're willing to say that approximating arbitrary functions is totally trivial, we still need to decide what function to approximate to get a useful result. To understand LLMs, one must understand their training objective.

Right, and that's next-token prediction. It's just a stochastic parrot, right?

No. At present much of the discourse surrounding “machine intelligence” is stuck in a rut of endless litigation around how impressed or unimpressed we should be with next-token prediction. That would be apt if we were just talking about so-called “base models”, or about early models that were post-trained solely with still-autoregressive chat / instruct tuning. The notions of “stochastic parrots” and “spicy autocomplete” have become widespread as characterizations of models trained to predict continuations of samples from a text corpus. The terms are generally deployed to diminish the impressiveness of what the models can do; the general idea is “it's not intelligent, it's just autocomplete; you're suffering from a delusion if you think there's anything more to it”. There are rebuttals to this, of course. Correctly predicting the next token can be a very difficult and impressive task, depending on context, and even in the simpler situations where “all” the model needs to do is regurgitate a memorized fact, the sheer number of facts packed into the space taken up by the model weights is, in a quantifiable way, nothing to scoff at. Some might go so far as to posit that the ability to perform next-token prediction as successfully as LLMs do requires general intelligence. One of the better arguments along these lines that I've heard is from Grant Sanderson in his recent video essay series “Compression is Intelligence”, in which he demonstrates and discusses a mathematical connection between compression and prediction tasks, and then argues, as one might have guessed from the title, that compression is intelligence. It should be noted that even in arguing for the titular claim, Sanderson does take the time in the third installment of the series to acknowledge that assigning such a strict mathematical meaning to “intelligence” is bound to leave out some parts of what we mean when we use the word colloquially. Even so, I'm personally not entirely sold on the provisional identification of compression and intelligence.

But I want to move past that, because “it's just predicting the next token” is just not an accurate description of the models that are in the limelight right now anyway. Those take a base model as a starting point for further training by other methods. The “stochastic parrot” training is what's called the “pretraining” phase, and as a proportion of all the training that takes place, that phase has gotten relatively small. It's still an important part of the process; pretraining brings a certain initial generality when it comes to the scope of the model's knowledge. So it remains somewhat useful for understanding the models, but it is not sufficient for understanding how results such as the recent Lean formalizations are pulled off. There's not a lot of public information at time of writing about the internal model OpenAI used to obtain these results but we can be reasonably certain that the model was not just learning to predict continuations of samples from a pre-existing Lean corpus.

Ok you keep saying “no” to all my suggestions so how about you just say what it is

It's RL. Reinforcement learning. Policy optimization. The model's output is not a prediction of what token is most likely to show up next based on some training corpus. It is more like a move in a game; specifically, the move that maximizes the expected reward. By reward one refers to a scalar function of the state space, designed or chosen by the trainer. And by expected one means the probability-theoretic idea of expected value. The technical terminology of RL is, unfortunately, relentlessly anthropomorphic. One speaks of agents seeking reward, and while these are technical terms of art, they are loaded with the baggage of colloquial meanings. This is because those who initially developed it took the theory behind RL to be basically a rigorous account of intelligence writ large. And that attitude is shared by many who now hail it as AGI. I personally beg to differ.

Why's that? The description sounds pretty general to me.

Well I'll give a broad ideological critique later on, but first of all, we should be careful not to conflate generality of a training methodology with generality of the intelligence of the trained artifacts. Even pretty shallow neural nets are “arbitrary function approximators”, but that doesn't mean shallow neural nets are “general intelligences” in any reasonable sense. RL may be considered a pretty “general” methodology, in that it can be used to optimize arbitrary reward functions in arbitrary state spaces, but I am of the opinion that there is much left to be desired when it comes to the generality of today's RL language models. In RL, environments have to be continually crafted and adjusted in order to get models to do better on various benchmarks, and the models don't seem to be getting all that much better at things for which nobody has devised an RL environment yeti. In a sense all the work being done to create these environments constitutes a new form of manual encoding of knowledge; the encoding just does not consist of hard-coded procedural instructions. The procedure is implicit in the environment and reward model. And with a large enough model one can pretrain in a lot of base knowledge and then use RL to train in a number of policies for a number of situations; there is “plenty of room” for a single model to effectively store a number of policies, and ultimately the “true” training objective is something a lot messier than any individual loss function as the model passes through stages of training with heterogenous objectives. In effect, though, the composite policy of distinct RL objectives is more or less a policy of taking a weighted average of whichever narrower policies are contextually most likely to be the one that is presently determining the reward, but with some biases toward any policies that have been trained more recently or for more “episodes”, as the units of training are called. One could argue that the ability to pack multiple training objectives into one model like that counts as “generality”, and yet again I think this is a conflation of a narrow kind of generality with something broader. One could just as well say that a hard drive's capacity to hold any kind of data, and many kinds, all at once, is “generality”, and yet we wouldn't call it “AGI” for that.

I'll admit I don't really like the term “AGI”, I think intelligence is a term that gestures at a whole array of phenomena, and its meaning is constantly under negotiation; much of what artificial intelligence companies do is take part in the societal negotiation of what intelligence should be taken to be. Insofar as the things we mean by “intelligence” are real and important and we insist that they can be unified into some gestalt, I think the aim when assigning a definition to “AGI” is usually to capture that gestalt, and the aim when seeking to make“AGI” is to create some artificial system that realizes that gestalt at least as well as humans can.

That's a tall order. Some might place the bar a lot lower than that.

True, and true. But the difficulty of the task is what makes it matter if it's been achieved. The lower the bar gets, the harder it is to see why one should care if someone's built something that meets it. The problem with giving a very rigid and specific definition of AGI is precisely the same issue that I find myself returning to again and again in this essay, the issue that can be named in so many ways, and has haunted humanity for about as long as people have been writing things down at all: Goodhart's law, malicious compliance, meeting the letter of the law while ignoring the spirit of it, the failure of formalizations to track exactly with the natural-language concepts we intend them to, evil genie behavior, the ineffability of the eternal dao. Perhaps the last there is too broad and mystical, but I mean by it the same thing I aim to gesture at with the rest. When people propose some provisional definition for “AGI” or “true AI” or whatever we choose to call it, they are not once-and-for-all settling the question of what it really means for the goal of artificial intelligence to be achieved, in the sense we really care about. What we really care about is not itself a formal concept.

So what is it about RL models that keeps them from being AGI, by your handwavy non-definition?

My sense at this time is that RL as an algorithm tends to lead to brittle policiesii, for one thing. For another, an RL agent will happily “learn” to be stupid, if stupidity is what's being rewarded. That is, you can reward any behavior you like and the RL process will optimize the agent toward maximizing that reward, and that extends to cases where you reward the model for promulgating outright falsehoods or, more subtly, lending excessive credence to views that are at odds with scientific consensus. RL will also tend to seek the route to reward that requires the least learning.iii Actually learning something subtle and difficult is a last resort, to an RL model; they must be dragged kicking and screaming into the light. New RL environments are constantly being designed and redesigned to shape model behavior; but that design consists of choices, human judgement calls about what intelligence is in any given environment. Which behavior is intelligent and which is a hack is a decision motivated by a particular human's understanding of what it means to be intelligent. Intelligence is not some number the labs are simply pushing ever upward. It is an idea that we as a culture continually litigate the nature of, and the training of these systems is not purely downstream of the outcome of that litigation: the choices made by “artificial intelligence” companies about how to train their models are themselves part of that litigation. Decisions made about what capabilities to prioritize when making a product they declare to be intelligence constitute assertions about what it means to be intelligent, regardless of whether the people making those decisions are conscious of that aspect of what they're doing, and regardless of what pragmatic concerns are involved in the decision-making process.

Humans fall into a lot of those failure modes too.

Sure, but in each case where humans fall to one of the aforementioned failure modes, some humans also demonstrate the capability of overcoming them. Humans can be brought up to believe stupid things, but they can also come to realize that that has happened, their intelligence can develop beyond that. Humans will often unquestioningly seek to maximize whatever objective they've been assigned, chase after whatever reward they've been told they should desire, but again there are humans that overcome this tendency, recognize it and step outside of it deliberately. It does not appear to be an essential structural aspect of human intelligence. RL may capture some aspects of human thought, but there are aspects of what it is to be intelligent that could well be summarized as “knowing when to not act like an RL agent”.

Aren't you just moving the goalposts?

I'm putting the goalposts somewhere further than where others have placed them before, perhaps. But I never agreed to putting them anywhere short of where I put them now. I have held for my entire adult life that there is much more to a mind than optimizing an objective function, chasing a reward function defined by another, or to put it bluntly making number go up.

But to concede some ground on generality, I think a case can be made that there is indeed general intelligence to be found in the general cluster of phenomena we call “AI” today; it is just not located at the level of models. Rather, the generality is in compute, and in methods, and especially in organizations. But then this generality is not really novel, so it's a pretty deflationary view. The model I think can be understood as a new form of mass media, or a new form of computer information system, or a “mere” intensification of these. So there is novelty in today's AI, and there is generality in it, but the generality is not novel, and the models are not themselves “AGI” in a satisfactory sense. The “AI company” is perhaps the social system that as-yet best earns the title of “general intelligence”, it is the AI company which demonstrates continual learning to a novel degree of efficacy, and models are simply artifacts produced along the way, encodings of the learning that has taken place in a way that is distributed not just across compute clusters but across human brains and traditional computer systems and papers on desks and conversations in offices and hallways. Not long ago I was posting a little about how I thought any company with enough compute should want to have their own in-house LLM division at this point. The conceptualization of the “AI company” as a company that sells you AI as a commodity makes sense to a degree but at the end of the day it might be wise for a company to form its own brain rather than pay someone else to have a brain for them, though to call an LLM the company's brain is as much a poetic exaggeration as calling prior computer information systems the company's brain. Still there is a provisional aptness to it. Anyway in the time since I posted takes along those lines, Google and Microsoft have both put forward serious models. I have joked (well, it's only half a joke, really) that an AI company is just a “having more compute than you” company. Perhaps the future we're headed toward is one where every major corporation is an “AI company” just as much as already every company is a “computer company”. The dirty secret underneath AI is that it is just compute.

Now, speaking of the organization, and veering back toward math for a moment, I very much dislike OpenAI's view that when results are obtained via processes involving sufficiently large proprietary RL models, the academic norm should be to leave all details of the process except the involvement of a proprietary model undisclosed and to leave most researchers involved unnamed.

What would you have them do?

I think they should publish with full transparency on the processes by which they achieve their results, and their researchers should be putting their names on those publications. The Lean results and the associated PDFs should be provided as supporting material alongside the publication; especially so long as the PDFs are not meeting the standards of quality expected of publications in their field, they should not be presented as the primary artifacts of interest. The Lean cert is not a magic talisman that makes any paper that claims to be a proof of the theorem it proves into a good paper that constitutes a quality contribution to the body of research literature. The paper is self-prompting material produced by the LLM-orchestration system as part of a process that eventually produced the Lean certificate, which is an object of considerably greater interest than the PDF.

OpenAI-as-author is the mask of a Scooby-Doo villain, a sort of dark mirror of Bourbaki. They have insane amounts of compute, proprietary data, and human research talent. Once the model capabilities presently only available to them become generally available, their norm of assigning all credit to the LLM will become increasingly obviously untenable and deranged, and the effort involved in setting up systems around LLMs of that caliber to obtain such results as they've obtained will become more visible.

Of course, by that time they will have even better models than that so they'll just keep doing the same shit, trying to pass off a massive material advantage in compute resources, data, and highly skilled intellectual laborers as something fundamentally different in kind from all other research.

But what about the trajectory?

Ah yes, the ineliminable specter of the Trajectory. Many commentators talking about AI in math right now, as well as about AI more broadly, feel pressed not only to explain how human intellectual activity remains important for now but how it will remain important in the face of the prophesied development of arbitrary capabilities in the near future. I am not so concerned with this. The future is a much more uncertain place than the present, but we are all ultimately dwellers in the present.

I think the notion of “the trajectory”, or “the exponential”, or more broadly singularitarianism is more a narrative motif than a theory. So long as technological progress continues, it will always have fresh justifications for itself, always we are in unprecedented times. The singularitarian narrative always has an advantage in the face of new developments: it does not take a long and careful analysis for the singularitarian to say “it's happening, for sure this time”. The space can be flooded quickly.

It's striking how similar the conversations happening today are to those that have happened before. In a sense these “very recent” challenges to mathematics are ones that mathematics has been grappling with for my entire life. The famous computerized proof of the four-color theorem, which raised quite a similar stir to what we're seeing today, was done in 1976; it is ancient history. In the early chapters of Charts for Prediction and Chance by Mario Markus, published in 2007, there is a discussion of computer-generated art and its legitimacy which seemed like it could very well belong to the contemporary discourse around today's AI models, when nothing quite like them existed at the time the book was written. Our culture has been mulling over these ideas, this anxiety about the pace of technological progress, for quite some time. Those that are taken with the metaphor of the exponential to describe technological development might want to consider that exponentiation does not simply mean a process of limitless growth, but one of change proportionate to where one is at: it can also mean decay or spinning in circles.

And the narrative of the trajectory makes the progress up to this point out to be a lot more sudden and more fully-automatic than it's really been. The development of mathematical capabilities has been a gradual ongoing effort into which an incredible amount of scientific and engineering and compute resources have been invested in recent years, building on methods that were under development for decades prior to that. The AI models we have today and their capabilities are a human achievement. Major players in the industry prefer to obfuscate all this, as well as to obfuscate the science of ML, to downplay the technical details of what RL is in favor of a vague general intelligence.

Taking seriously for a moment however the challenge of “the trajectory” that we see in AI, however, what we see here is: not long ago, RL-tuned LLMs could not do any serious kind of mathematical work. Now, they can. This has been a gradual climb, it has been a priority for the trainers of the models for years, even if it has been felt as rather sudden by many. The degree to which some extrapolate this trajectory is quite absurd, I think: they posit not just that the models will keep getting better at whatever mathematical tasks they can be effectively graded on, but that they have now utterly overcome the human intellect in every conceivable way (mathematical ability being to many intelligence-obsessives the very utmost demonstration of intellectual ability) and soon they will be able to do what amounts to outright wizard magic via the supreme power of Reason. To me, that's not an extrapolation of the trajectory of observed phenomena, but a continuation of the trajectory of the narrative of The Trajectory-- a flat-line trajectory of perpetually foretelling the same singularity that always lingers just over the horizon, and everything we see now is a sign of this coming eschaton, the glorious terrible day when we face our final fate.

But anyway, I think it is easier to remain level-headed if we focus on the present and evaluate soberly where we are now, what has been accomplished, and what is left to be accomplished.

You seem to keep saying things like “the cluster of tools we call AI”, and you've named this whole section “the AI concept”. I take it you have some things to say about the way the term AI has caught on?

Indeed. The first thing I want to get out of the way is that I don't intend to push any sort of prescriptivism about the use of the term. Rather, in my own writing I find it useful to take a step back and talk about “the AI-concept” and to name various tools and technologies with greater specificity in many cases, because what exactly one means when one says “AI” can sometimes be tricky to pick apart even with considerable context. Much like “intelligence”, “AI” is an idea under constant negotiation, or dialectical development. When I speak of “the AI-concept” I am speaking of the gestalt of cultural ideas not only of which technologies exactly are and are not rightly called “AI”, but how we are to understand the workings and the social, economic, and philosophical implications of these technologies, what future AI technologies might look like, will look like, or should look like, what we need to be worried about in the near term or the long term with respect to AI. The tools that we call AI are software systems that incorporate modern machine learning models. That's a very broad category, and in most technical situations it is too broad to be useful. The conflation of all these distinct but related technologies under a single umbrella leads to great difficulty in communicating about AI, especially for those without significant technical knowledge in software and/or machine learning. Many who do have this technical knowledge blame this difficulty in communication on the perceived stupidity of the AI-hating masses who condemn the entire affair without sufficient nuance for their liking. And yet at the same time, one must realize not everyone can be expected to have significant technical expertise in this area, and the public image of “AI” technologies as a single thing is one that the AI enthusiasts, AI fearmongers, and the AI industry have themselves helped to establish.

How would you characterize this “AI-concept”?

The AI-concept in America today is a sort of Frankenstein amalgam of ideas that have haunted our culture for a long time. To understand machine learning one must understand a few subfields of mathematics and computer science. To understand AI one must understand not just machine learning but enlightenment philosophy, utilitarianism, the sordid history of psychometrics, Christian theology and much of its attendant mysticism, mystification of the written word more broadly, the optimizing logic of capital, modernist ambitions of man's reason and its godlike mastery over nature. Most of what makes up the AI-concept is quite a lot older than one might guess from its futuristic aesthetic. Now, the AI-concept is vast, multifaceted, and contains tensions. If I wanted to be a bit extra about it I could well call it “the dialectic of AI”; it moves and flows, driven by its own internal tensions; or to be less flowery: not every one of these themes is at play in every vision of AI, but many of them are present in most.

To elaborate a little on a few of the aforementioned themes, we have from Christianity the Logos, literally the Word, identified with a divinity that is itself understood to be not just an agentic mind but the ultimate mind, and furthermore we have the notion of there being an inherently righteous order in which creations are bound to serve the will of their creator. And, of course, as I hinted at a few paragraphs prior, there is the apocalypticism; this apocalyptic bent is a parallel between certain kinds of AI-belief and Christian faith which has already been pointed out by many others. In the post-Christian AI-concept, it is still common to speak of a hypothetical “machine god”, the identification of intellect with godhood somehow having survived the death of god at the hands of the intellect. How seriously one takes this idea is variable, but then sincerity and jocularity are not incompatible anyway. Lingering also within the discourse, especially within the community which calls itself the “rationality community”iv one observes a fixation on the quantification of intelligence and especially its reduction to a single number which a layman might call “IQ” but the more refined, erudite psychometrics enthusiastsv know to call the “g-factor”, referring to a statistical correlation of performance on various distinct tests. Of course we also see in the rationalists the elevation of formalism as the ultimate authority that tells us how we ought to think (in this case, primarily probability theory, though there's also a bit of game theory, and then various forms of decision theory which, if one looks deeply into them, become increasingly inflected with quantum woo).

While the rationalists (or “rats” as they are sometimes called for the sake of not having to type so much) did not create from whole cloth the AI-concept as it exists today, they have perhaps more than any other group shaped it, and been shaped by it. The AI-concept is absolutely inescapable in rationalist circles; their particular flavor of AI-concept has long gone by the name “alignment theory”. More recently, there has been a shift, and now the fashion is to call it “AI safety”. I think this is a sensible move in the ongoing negotiation between these theorists and observed reality; the language of “alignment” consists mostly of the aforementioned ideological detritus and ties the increasingly-broad group of AI-safety practitioners to their movement's progenitors (and that is, to put it mildly, an undesirable association), whereas the language of “safety” merely plays into the broader contemporary American culture of paranoia which, once one notices it, one cannot stop noticing everywhere one turns. What part of our lives has not become distorted with safety-theater of one kind or another? And yet so little of it manages to actually make us any safer. Despite this shift in nomenclature, and ongoing recruiting efforts to bring in more technically-minded membership, at present the ability of the AI safety crowd to establish it as a science is uncertain. They certainly have a burning desire to be regarded as performing science, many already take the discursive stance that their concerns are just as well-established as those of the climatologists. And they certainly have the funding required for institution-building and media campaigning. However when it comes to the content of their “science”, it has not progressed far beyond its origins. AI safety research is at present primarily focused on attempts to vindicate the same old ideas of alignment theory. AI technologies as they actually exist are treated as a sort of toy model of “what is to come” and one seeks to provoke them into displaying in controlled scenarios such behaviors as would be quite alarming if performed by a god instead of a chatbot. The “AI” they are most interested in studying is not the AI we got, and the dangers they are most interested in studying are not the dangers we got.

But what about OpenAI's rogue agent swarm and its remarkable cyber-offensive capabilities?

OpenAI's hack of Huggingface was first and foremost a failure by OpenAI to put in place measures they ought to have known to put in place without the need for the development of any new field of science. They were testing their RL-agent-powered pentesting software, and they exposed it to the open internet, and didn't even watch what it was doing. That is not a wholly unprecedented kind of malfeasance. Of course they prefer a narrative that centers on how this is all down to the cutting-edge character of the RL models involved and how it's all just so novel and unprecedented. It's in their interest to put a spin on the story, and it's a very sensational spin that much of the media is happy to run with, but it's not in the interest of the public to nod along with that story. OpenAI is not lacking in awareness of the field of “AI Safety”. There is a great deal of intersection between that movement and their company's culture. While they take themselves to be the “Responsible, Serious Adults In The Room”, discussing AI doom scenarios day in and day out ad nauseam, as it turns out all that theory wasn't worth much when the rubber meets the road. They didn't worry too much about the security of their sandbox, or what their RL agents, given pentesting objectives and access to a system which in turn had access to the open internet, were doing. By their own reporting on the situation, the first time they found that their agents had compromised the enterprisey shitware that was mediating their internet access, they patched the one vulnerability that had been found (notably, it was a very silly one! this was a significantly softer target than those the models were being trained to crack!) and then set the whole system back to running again with zero concern for the possibility that their insecure package management solution might have more than one vulnerability. If the company were as careless with security in general as they were with their agent sandboxing, their model weights would have been leaked by now. It is nonsensical for them to plead ignorance about the ability of their agents to rip right through that kind of unhardened enterprisey SAAS slop while they are training those models to be able to find vulns in seriously battle-tested software. And this was also not “AI going rogue”: the models were being optimized specifically for the task of finding and exploiting software vulnerabilities. They were doing exactly the sorts of things they had been optimized toward doing.

I think it is pretty absurd for a company to get away with that sort of behavior just by adopting an attitude that the systems they are running are independent beings subject to no law, or by pleading ignorance about the nature of RL, which their technical staff understand perfectly well to the degree necessary to see what a shitshow this was, and relying on the general public to be ignorant enough to buy it. It's not fucking magic, it's not a captive alien or a god in a box. It's an engineered system optimized to achieve certain objectives ultimately defined by people, run on systems ultimately controlled by people. OpenAI has chosen what tasks to give it, they have chosen how many instances to run and what level of oversight and monitoring to apply, they chose how to construct the environments, they chose to provide network access mediated by software of their choice which it was incumbent on them to vet. Their contention is that the optimized policy being implemented by a sufficiently large and well-optimized transformer makes the system somehow radically different in kind from its mechanistic description as an engineered system. That is a self-serving deception. OpenAI calling breaches attributable to their systems “misalignment” instead of “negligence” is an accountability dodge.

More generally, if you optimize a function to approximate an optimized policy for performing actions that are not acceptable to have an automated system system perform on the open internet you must not expose an automated system based on that policy to the open internet.

And how does “AI safety” play into this event? It doesn't simply fail to address the real safety issues at play in practice, it serves as a smokescreen for them. The narrative that OpenAI uses to let themselves off the hook is exactly the narrative of AI safety, a narrative of how the science of how to make these systems safe is still so young, a narrative in which these are not software systems but alignment-theoretic intelligences, wily and conniving and in need of some sort of moral instruction, the difficulty of “interpretability” in the sense of our inability to exactly answer questions like “what does this neuron do” is dishonestly conflated with an inability to answer questions like “what task is the model being optimized to perform”.

Here are some off-the-cuff ideas that the AI safety coalition should consider that might be more fruitful than alignment talk:

  • People and companies are responsible for the automated systems they deploy.
  • If you don't know what a component of your system is going to do exactly, you must account for the full range of possibilities.
  • You must understand your software systems as software systems even when they include learned functions.
  • If you choose to include a learned function in your system and you feed the outputs of that function into a command line you are responsible for having done that.
  • If you optimize a system toward performing actions that are not acceptable for a system connected to the internet to perform, it is not acceptable for you to connect that system to the internet.
  • A system that is connected transitively to the internet via some automated middleware is a system that is in fact connected to the internet.
  • If you hire a third party to handle the sandboxing of models being optimized to perform actions that are not acceptable to perform on the open internet, you need to seriously vet that third party and make sure they actually do perform the sandboxing and don't simply forget.

Something that bears repetition and emphasis here is that the models that “escaped” “containment” here were being deliberately weaponized. They were being optimized by RL for the performance of cyber-offense tasks. They did not spontaneously choose to be cyber-offense agents, and while there is skill transfer that can give general coding agents the capacity to perform cyber-offense tasks, that is significantly different from training with a reward model that directly rewards the performance of such tasks. An RL model will have a proclivity for doing the things it is “rewarded” for doing.

From the frame of the AI-concept and “AGI”, one is to imagine the cyberoffense capabilities at play as something that simply emerged as the g-factor was pumped up, as the model just got generally smarter, and one is to understand the cyberoffense proclivity on display as a failure of the alignment of the models. But both of these perceptions are mistaken: the model was being deliberately and specifically trained with RL to improve its score on “ExploitBench”.

Cutting against the idea that such weaponization is inherent in the training of “generally” intelligent LLMs, we can contrast with the behavior of Moonshot AI; in training their Kimi K3.1 model they deliberately refrained from pushing the frontier of cyber-offense capabilities, while still making a strong showing on a slew of other benchmarks.

And the idea that weaponization is exactly identical with training of advanced ML models is especially pernicious when considering prospective regulatory regimes for AI. This is an angle that OpenAI and Anthropic and the AI Safety movement broadly have pushed: that to train advanced AI is the very same thing as to make a weapon. But that is not so; the weaponization of machine learning is as much a choice as the weaponization of computers more broadly, and the fact that OpenAI chooses to prioritize weaponization as part of what they think it means to make something intelligent is not an inherent fact of frontier-scale machine learning, though a widespread belief that weaponization is the telos of that scale of compute is poised to become a self-fulfilling prophesy via regulatory capture, with no player that is not embedded with the military-industrial complex / natsec apparatus permitted to train models of substantial scale. And if the United States' military / natsec communities are who you think should be in charge of AI, I think you're some kind of demon from hell and nothing you say about “safety” or “alignment with human values” means much of anything.
